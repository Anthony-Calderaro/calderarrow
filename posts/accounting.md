Accounting is the language of business.
Accounting allows us to convert apples to organges, oranges to apples, hardware to software, machinary to daffadils. It allows us to have a universal set of rules and terminology to quantify and compare two organizations which have seemingly different operations, sizes, and purposes.

Difference from finance and economcs
Accounting tells you where you are and how you got here. Finance is concerned about where you could go. It's inherently more subjective, and therefore finance as an industry tends to lean more optistic or pessimistic depending on the temperment of its practicitioners. Economics is finance if you throw psychology into the mix.

I enjoyed learning finance and economics. They're sexier, more interesting. They give one the feeling that they can predict the future, which puts order to chaos. AP Economics was my first introduction to this field, back in my senior year of high school. But while it was entertaining to muse about different theories, I wanted more fact. I didn't want to trust black boxes and models, I wanted to validate them. Finance and economics are abstractions on accounting, and I wanted to get as close to the ground as I could get. It wouldn't be until my sophmore year of college when I began my dive into accounting.

In accounting, you learn about the basic [rules](acocunting), the syntax, and the general idea of accounting, which is to make sure that all of the transactions a business has are accounted for. We validate that they are accounted for properly from the most important rule in accounting, which is that Assets must always equal Liabilities plus equity (A = L + E). Each transaction -- any transaction -- any organization can make can be placed neatly or forcibly wedged into at least one of these 3 categories. Assets are anything that can be used to generate revenue, liabilities are items which generate expenses, and equity is the net difference of all the revenues and expenses over time. If a transaction is missing, the equation won't balance. If a transaction is misstated, the equation won't balance. It has built in checks for us.

A large portion of accounting is learning how to categorize and name transactions for clarity when discussing them with others or presenting them on financial statements. For our purposes, it really doesn't matter what you name them, as long as you can identify which of the three buckets a transaction impacts, and quantify the amounts, then you can do accounting TM.

It doesn't matter if a businesses is farming soybeans or creating software. We can classify each transaction, and compare the organizations based on the relationship between the Assets, Liabilities, and Equity. Ratios allow us to account for scale differences. We convert every transaction into a least common denmominator, A, L, or E.

# Humans are Corporations

But these laws don't just apply to corporations or governments. They apply to people too. After all, an artist is a company of 1, where he is the CEO, Art Director, Head of Sales, and office manager. He may have to take out a loan (liability) to procure supplies (Assets). As he paints, he will incurr expenses in the process of creating his inventory (assets) to later sell (revenue). Humans are bound by the same laws of accounting as businesses. Sometimes, people get into financial trouble by forgetting this, and allow their expenses to outpace their revenues. Othertimes, circumstances are forced upon them which wipe out large assets, or force them to take on huge liabilities. Whatever the reason, the financial difficulties won't go away until the equation is corrected.

But why do we even care? Why even use money? What is the purpose?
Take ourselves way back. Born into the wilderness. WE have no concept of money, no assets, liabilities or expenses. There is no trade, no concept of property. If I want shelter, I cut down trees and build a cabin. If I want food, I hunt with weapons I crafted from rocks and sticks. I am truly free.

The problem with this type of life is that we are inherently limited in what we can do, because we cannot excel at everything. How long does it take a human to fasten an axe, chop down a tree and fasten a roof? How long does it take to fasten weapons and hunt? In modern times, how long would it take me to mine iron and coal to build into steel? How long would it take me to build a car from scratch? It would be free of money, but not of time. These problems would not matter if we were immortal. Given an infinite amount of time, I could eventually learn how to make a car from scratch. I could also invent rocket ships and nuclear power and anything else we as humans have ever created. But since we only have a few decades, we are highly limited in what we can do in our brief time. Being able to specialize on something means I can focus on it.

Ideally, it would be better for me to focus on a few things to get really good at, and make them for everyone. This way, I could trade my surplus with other people for their surplus. My hats for their gloves. My computer for their software. By spending the same amount of time, we are able to become wealthier, because more is accessible to us than what we could have achieved by ourselves. But if I make shoes and you want gloves, how can I trade with you? I would need to find a third party who wants my shoes and your gloves, and is willing to trade you something you want. What if there were a common tool we could use to represent a shoe and a glove, and trade that instead? This is where the idea for currency comes in. If I know that 1 shoe = 1 dollar = 1 glove, I can trade my dollar for either a glove or a shoe, without needing to bring the shoe or glove along with me to trade. We can discount the good or service to a currency, and use that to trade instead.

But how many gloves is a shoe worth? How many computers is a car worth? How do we value these goods? How do we adjust the pricing to account for changes in society and technology? Not all goods and services are of equal value to all people. To <bold>account</bold> for this in the most dynamic way possible, we relate each good or service to a unit of effort or time. Making a cup of coffee and making a car require different levels of effort, resources, and time. This influences the currency value assigned to them. A father may love spending time with his toddler, and love it so much that he is willing to do it for free. But if he wants to go out on a friday evening, he'll have to pay the neighborhood babysitter a small fortune to watch his child for a few hours. The babysitter's time could be better spent elsewhere (perhaps going out himself), and therefore carries a value to it. If you want access to his services, you have to compensate him accordingly.

And this hints at the ultimate focus of accounting, which is not so much to account for money or currency, but to account for time. If I'm lucky, I have 80 years to live. What do I want to do during that 80 year period? What can I do? I can't build a home, learn rocket science, and go to the moon, but if I become a professional astronaut, maybe people will pay me to learn rocket science and go to the moon, and I can use some of my earnings to afford a mortgage. This would be impossible to do alone in a thousand years, but by working together as part of a greater community, it can be done in a human's lifespan.

Time is the most valuable resource any of us have. We have an unknown amount of it, which is exhaserbated by the fact that it is a finite amount. Currency is a representation of the value of time. $1 can be equal to 5 minutes of a barista making me espresso, or 5 seconds of brain surgery. Accumulating money means giving your time to others, doing things other people want from you, which we refer to as work. Spending money means receiving the rewards of other people's work. Wealth is not a permanent fixture. Wealth can ebb and flow as ones time-savings deplete or grow.

# Time is money

Time is more than money. Time can be converted into money, but it can also be converted into things money can't buy. More time with a loved one, experiences of a lifetime. Money is not time, in that you can't buy more years of your life, although you can buy other people's time, their time is only good while you yourself are still alive.

So if you're interested in learning how to account for time, and all the nuances involved with valuing, assessing, measuring, and comparing time, accounting may be a great field for you.
