## Why have them?

Organizations -- not just companies -- use values to communicate information and clarify a mission. Values are related to a mission, but distinct. A mission gives you the destination. Values tell you about those pursuing a mission.

For companies, values paint a picture of the ideal employee, customer, or shareholder, who would support the corporate mission. To be good, values have to communicate useful information about how the company goes about achieving its mission. Values are similar to tactics, but tactics focus on what people _do_ whereas values focus on what people _are_. A good value can be unethical, and some very ethical values turn out to be quite bad. The key factor is whether the value is useful, not whether it fits an arbitrary moral compass.

## Bad Values

> We value customer service

Intuitively, you probably see this as a bad value. If not, consider whether you're evaluating the conclusion of the value (that customer service is good to value) rather than the usefulness of knowing this value.

Here's another one:

> We value not committing murder.

I find that having an example of what not to do helps me understand the characteristics that make it bad. And once I know what makes a value bad, I can do the opposite to arrive at good values.

What makes these values bad is that taken in isolation, they do not help you decide whether to support an organization or not. You would need additional information. And because they are insufficient on their own, they are useless. And my definition of a bad value is one which is useless.

Imagine if you were considering buying coffee from a shop with a sign outside saying:

> We value good customer service and not killing people.

Wouldn't this seem...obvious? Of course you should value customer service: you exist to serve customers. Of course you should value not killing people: that's just table stakes.

Now, if right next door was another coffee shop with a sign that said:

> We value killing people and poor customer service.

Having the former's sign of values would be of use to you. It would become a way to help you as a consumer, investor, regulator, or potential employee decide which company you wanted to support. It helps you decide between choices.

Now we have the beginnings of a definition: A value is useful because it helps others choose. A value is good because it is useful without added context.

If a company's values requires me to compare it to another company, they are not as good as values which would allow me to make my decision without requiring additional context. In reality, we rarely see such divisive values of companies so close together -- but consider how often you see multiple restaurants on the same block. How often do you see car dealerships across the street from each other? How often do you see dozens of brands of toilet paper or cartons of eggs or almost any other product or service out there with such divisive values? How do you decide who to patronize? If you're a potential employee, how do you decide which to work for?

It would help if they had good corporate values.

## What makes a good value?

I have a 3-step heuristic for determining whether a value is good:

1. Could a reasonable person align with this value?
2. Next, negate the value.
3. Could a reasonable person could align with the negated value?

If the answer to 1 or 3 is `no`, the original value is bad.

Let's take an example. Say we want to determine whether the following value is good:

> We value customer service

1. Could a reasonable person align with this value? Sure, I think most people would say that they would value a company with good customer service. Customers would shop there, prospective employees would want to work there, investors would want to invest.

2. Negate the value:

> We do not value customer service

3. Could a reasonable person align with that value? What type of company would willingly admit this? What self-respecting consumer would support a company openly declaring their anti-customer-service stance?[0] If two identical stores were side-by-side, only one had this posted on their door, would we expect them to receive business? Probably not. [1]

No reasonable competitor would ever declare the opposite value, that they do not value customer service. Because no one would never differentiate themselves using the negated value, this value does not help us make a decision. Since it is not a useful value, we can conclude that "We value customer service" is a bad value.

Let's look at another:

> We value quality over price

1. Could a reasonable person align with this value? Absolutely. Some customers may be willing to pay more for higher quality goods. Artisans may prefer to work somewhere that values their craftsmanship. Investors may prefer to invest in a brand that produces higher quality items in smaller batches.

2. Negate the value:

   > We value price over quality

3. Could a reasonable person align with that value? Absolutely. Some customers may be price-sensitive and not care as much about quality. A potential employee may prefer the challenge of delivering a product or service at the lowest cost possible. An investor may want to invest in a company which mass produces items at scale.

These values provide useful, discernable information to people. We can conclude that it is a good value.

Interestingly, the negated value is also a good value. As a general observation, if a value passes this test, typically the negated value is good as well. It actually seems to be that either the original and negated values are both good values or they are both bad.

## Why do companies use bad values?

So if it's so easy to determine a good value, why would a company use a bad value?

I boiled it down to a few options:

1. Pride
2. Ignorance
3. Fear

### Pride

Companies want to virtue signal and boast about how great they are. They use these values as an arrogant form of marketing by _telling_ you that they're good and hoping you don't call their bluff, rather than _showing_ you how they're good, and allowing you to arrive at your own conclusion.

### Ignorance

Sometimes companies have bad values because they don't know what their company actually values. Some low-end executive or outside marketing company is tasked with creating a set of corporate values, and they come up with a list of values that _sound_ nice, but are useless.

> We value integrity
> We value teamwork
> We value accountabiltiy and respect

Yes, integrity, teamwork, accountability and respect are all good traits to have, but they're too general. What makes your employees different from another companies? Why are your customers different from others? These companies don't know. When you read these values that come off like cliches, replace their list of values with:

> We don't know what we truly value in our organization.

### Fear

Companies may also have bad values because they're afraid to offend a segment of the population. Earlier, I mentioned that good values are useful because they allow people to decide between options. From a company's perspective, why would they ever want a potential customer, employee, or investor to choose someone else? Why use good values if they expose a potential difference -- a potential weaknesses? [2]

Because by definition, a good value always shows a weakness. If a football team says "we value offense over defense", they may not have the resources to build up a strong defense, which can be a weakness. If another team said "we value defense over offense", are they any more or less wrong? We can debate it, but the fact that its debatable shows the usefulness. The values provide insight into how the organization is going to strategize to achieve victory, but also expose potential weaknesses. There is no "perfect" strategy, so trying to avoid showing weakness through values is itself, a sign of weakness. If a coach comes into the media room and starts talking about how "we value offense and defense equally", take a look at his players. Either all his players are paid about average in the league, or he's lying.

A strong team runs on the field confident in their ability to outmaneuver and outperform their opponent, regardless of whether their opponent knows their strategy. Values get you better employees so you can execute better than opponents. Values get you customers so you can have money to pay the employees and investors for their work. Values create the environment for teams to execute. And teams that execute better, win.

##

Good values generally involve an arbitrary tradeoff. We value A, which gives us Y at he cost of Z. If someone can say We value B, which gives us Z at the cost of Y, that is likely a good value because it provides insight into their decisionmaking and culture.

A bad value is one which does not distinguish your group from others. For instance, saying we value hard work is stupid, because no one wants to associate with people who don't value working hard. It doesn't help people self-select for or against your culture.

If I made a company, my values would look something like this:

1. We value a remote-first work environment and pay for in-person events for team building and strategizing.
2. We prioritize tech debt over shipping new features. As tech debt accumulates when developing a feature.
3. We value work-life balance over top-of-the-line pay.
4. We value developing and promoting from within rather than hiring externally
