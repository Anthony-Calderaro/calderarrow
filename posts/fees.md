Background, wall street is suits. They have ivy league backgrounds, business degrees. Why? The Quant firms recruit the smart kids. The IBs recruit sales folks with rich connections. Nice suits, nice offices, lavish lifestyles. Wolf of Wallstreet and Wall Street are hyperbolic, but not too far off the mark.

Assets under management is a terrible, horrid way to structure fees. If there is one takeaway you get from this post, never work with someone who charges a fee based on assets under management. 

Fee structure should be tied to performance. 

Background: Advisor fees warp incentives. One of the hardest aspects about hiring an advisor is that without proper background in finance, it's hard to gauge whether they are performing. i don't need to be a plumber to gauge my plumber's performance. Is my leaky sink still leaking? Do my lights turn on when I flip the switch? Does my air conditioner work during the summer? These have a tangible, visual component which allows us to measure performance and treat the HVAC, Plumber, and Electrician technicians as a black box of magic, who accept money in exchange for fixing things beyond our comprehension.

with finance, it's harder. A typical heuristic is if i give you money, and come back in a year, do i have more money? This is easy, but creates opportunities for disincentives. 
## Intro 
The only acceptable fee structure for any financial advisor is one in which no fee is ever applied to the principle. If any part of the fee is based on the assets under management or otherwise applied to the principle, do not use that advisor. 
The rest of this post will contain two parts. In part 1, I will outline my reasoning for this opinion, and in part 2, I will provide an outline on how to evaluate and compare financial adivsors, so you can determine if it is worth it to use one over the other, or just do it yourself.

## Part i: Fee structures
AUM: 1% (incentive to gain more money, not do anything)

On its face, this seems to make sense. If you hire a plumber, you should pay more per sink. If you have a bigger house, it makes sense for your HVAC tech to charge more. The increase in labor is more closely correlated with the increase in cost, and so it follows that more work = more cost, because each additional unit of labor increases the costs of time, supplies, and materials. 

That is not the case with finance. The marginal cost of moving $1,000,000 is almost nothing compared with moving $1,000. So it does not make sense to charge proportionally more for managing more money. 

You may think that having more money results in more work because you have access to more opportunities for investment. Not exactly. 
> For individual investors, it’s easy to buy shares. But institutions like Goldman Sachs or Berkshire Hathaway that have tens of billions to invest are simply too large to get in on these deals.
> If you have $350,000 to invest, you have the pick of the finest publicly-available value investments on the planet, no matter how small the market.
> If you have $350 billion, you’re relegated to expensive railroads and government bonds.
- https://www.schiffsovereign.com/investing/warren-buffett-its-a-huge-advantage-not-to-have-a-lot-of-money-19216/

But the most egregious aspects of a fee applied to AUM is that you can lose money when your advisor doesn't perform. If you hire a plumber to fix 3 sinks, and he does nothing and leaves you with 3 leaky sinks, you wouldn't pay him. And if he left you with 4 leaky sinks, you'd be furious. But when a fee is applied to the AUM, an advisor can do nothing and still collect their fee. If you have $100k invested, that advisor is getting paid $1k to do nothing. Even worse, they can make shoddy investment decisions and lose you money, and then charge you a fee for the privilege. 

There is an additional perverse incentive with this fee structure: it incentivizes the advisor to focus on sales instead of doing finanical research. This is more apparent with some examples:

Let's take an advisor who charges 1% of AUM. AUM has two components: the principle you start with, and the capital gain (or loss). This 1% fee is applied at the end of the year and applied to both the principle and capital gain. An average return of the market over the past 150 years is 9.381%, but for ease of calculation, let's round that up to 10%. If the advisor gets $100,000 from each client, earns 10% each year, the total AUM at the end of the year will be $110,000: $100,000 principle + $10,000 capital gains. Their fee would be $1,100, but notice that only $100 of that came from the gains, which is the part the advisor influenced. After all, you were the one who started with the $100,000. So in our example, over 90% of their fees came from the principle they obtained from you, any only 10% of their fee came from the work they put into investing your money. What would it look like if they earned 50%? $1500, 500 from capital gains, and $1000 from the principle. Even if they are 5x better than the market, two-thirds of their fee still comes from your principle. 

And now consider the amount of effort it would take to find investment opportunities that are 5x better than the market, compared to the amount of effort it would take to find more customers to give $100,000 to the portfolio. Warren Buffet's firm, Berkshire Hathaway, has gotten an average of 20% annually since inception, so what do you think is easier for your advisor: consistently double Berkshire's performance, or find one more person to sell to? Selling to other clients is not aligned with your incentive of investing your money to generate returns.

There's another problem with fees applied to AUM, which is that an advisor can do the bare minimum of performance to retain your business, and if you are not financially savvy enough to evaluate their performance, they can profit off your recurring business like a parasite. We'll talk more about evaluating performance in part 2. For now, let's see if there is a fee structure we can use to better align our incentives with our advisor's.

Hourly: (incentive to waste your time)
To ensure that your advisor spends their time doing investment research, why not pay them an hourly fee? This is a step in the right direction, because you're thinking about how to align their incentives with yours, but it trades one problem for others. Now you have to worry about them billing you excessive hours for no actual results. Imagine if an electrician came over to repair your lights, spent all day in your basement, then left without fixing them, telling you he needs more time. What you really care about is the result, not the time spent doing it. If anything, you should be willing to pay a premium to get it done in less time, not more time. So this fee structure is suboptimal.

fixed: incentive to not go the extra mile
If the risk of an hourly fee is that the advisor works slowly, why not pay them a fixed fee for the job? You could get quotes from advisors on how much their fees are relative to the performance they obtain. This addresses the issues of non-performance, focuses the advisor on doing work for you instead of selling to others, and provides an incentive for them to complete the work sooner, but creates an incentive for them to do the bare minimum. While better than the prior two fee models, it is not an ideal model, because you end up leaving money on the table in the form of lost earnings. You could attempt to resolve this by creating tiers of performance, so that there is an incentive to perform more and more, but you still end up with a situation where the adivsor is making a decision about whether the extra performance is worth it: if they don't think they can reach the next tier, they may not even try. 

Another concern with this model is that you are likely out the fixed fee no matter what happens at the end of the year. Investment gains or losses cannot be predicted with absolute certainty, so unless the advisor receives substantial losses, you will likely be bound to pay the fee, even if they did not outperform their peers or benchmarks. 


One other fee model we have not yet discussed is a comission based advisor who is paid to sell you products. This is apparent if the advisor does not charge you directly, as they may be receiving kick backs from the financial service providers to sell to you. This is a horrible scenario, in which your advisor is just a personal sales associate of the company(s) they shill. Do not work with any advisor who receives a comission -- from you or anywhere else -- as part of their work.

From these scenarios, we can make a list of requirements for our ideal fee structure:
- Fees should never be applied to the principle
- Fees should never be comission
- Fees should only be applied to performance
- Fees should always incentivize advisors to perform better
- Fees should be structured to account for down years, and enable an advisor to make good investment decisions for the time-horizon of the investor

### I'm not an adivsor, but if I were, here's how I'd do it
Let's take our original example. If you have $100,000 to invest, how can we create a fee structure to ensure that the advisor gets paid $1,000 (1% of AUM) while creating an incentive to outperform the market. The market performance will serve as our benchmark, and we can use the hitsorical average since inception, which includes the lowest economic depressions and the highest economic booms: 10%. We know that a reasonable upper-limit for personal financial advisement could be Berkshire Hathaway's 20%, so we would expect somewhere around a 15% is a reasonable performance annually. On $100k, a 15% return would be 15k, but only 5k would be better than the market benchmark. So that should be the base our fee is applied to. To end up with $1,000, the fee would be 20% of the capital gains beyond our 10% benchmark. In this way, an advisor only gets paid when they earn you money. If they earn 10% or less, their fee is 20% of $0. This ensures that they only get paid when they perform, which is the same standard we would have for a plumber, an electrician, or any other traditional job. The 20% may cause sticker shock to some, since it's 20x more than the 1% of AUM. But remember that the larger percentage is applied to a smaller base. The principle will remain untouched.

Why not a fixed fee? Because this creates an incentive for them to accept less of your money. We heard that it's actually harder to find investment opportnities when u have more money, andu  want an advisor who will maximize your yield regardless of how much you have to invest.You also retain the perverse incentive where they will only work as hard as they need to outperform the market. You may get 11% when another advisor wuold have gotten you 13%, so the money you save on the upfront fee is lost in the annual yield. 

While this fee structure would work for an investor, would it work for an advisor? It can with some additional refinement. One instance that needs to be accounted for is when the market has a downturn. If _everyone_ is losing money, is there an instance where the advisor should get paid? 

If we consider a higher gain as outperforming the market, then by the same logic, a lower loss would be seen as outperforming the market. If everyone lost 10%, losing 15% would be pretty bad. But losing 5% would be excellent. So our definition of "gain" should be relative to the benchmark. If the advisor loses 5% when the market lost 10%, that difference should be the base we apply the fee to. Even accounting for the fee, you still end up better off than if you had gone with the market. Let's look at an example:

$100,000 invested when the market loses 10% means you end up losing $10k. If an advisor loses 5%, they would have saved you $5k, and applying their 20% fee to that $5k results in $1k. Your total losses would be $5k from the loss plus $1k in the advisor fee, or $6k total. This 6% is still better than the benchmark loss of $10k. You are $4k better off. 

In fact, as long as the fee structure is less than 100%, you will always beat the market will this structure, while ensuring your advisor can earn a living during good times and bad. 

Now, some years the market will beat the advisor, just like _someone_ will win the lottery. Over a long enough time horizon, a good advisor should consistently outperform the market, like Berkshire Hathaway. To account for this, an advisor may make one of several changes:
1. They may increase the fee percentage from 20% up to as much as 99.99%
2. They may require a time commitment for you to invest with them

In the first scenario, you should be willing to accept any fee structure less than 100%, because as we saw in our prior example, you will always be better off. 

For the second requirement, increasing the time commitment should be done with caution. This limits your liquidity, which can be problematic if you find a better advisor. A maximum of 2-years is reasonable, but anything beyond that should result in a substantially reduced fee to compensate you for the opportunity cost and liquidity risk you take by entering into the agreement. 

Under this setup, I would suggest a fee structure of 40-70%. Anything less, and I would be concerned about the ability for my advisor to make an adequate living. Any more, and I would feel taken advantage of. An advisor-investor relationship is more like a partnership, and I can't see myself putting up all the capital for less than a third of the equity. 

So now we have a fee structure we will accept. But if two advisors offer the same percentage, which do u choose? How do u compare two advisors? We will discuss this in part two.

## Part II: Evaluating Financial Performance
The average rate of return we've been discussing is a good metric to begin with, but we it needs additional refinement in order to prevent distortion. For instance, if you are comparing two advisors and ask for their annual return, one may show you an astronomical return because they bought lottery tickets and happened to win. But we know that this advisor was just lucky, not savvy, so we need to adjust our metrics to account for luck.

Another way for looking at luck is to consider the risk of being unlucky. Lotteries are a form of gambling, and while there are varying definitions of gambling, a good heuristic is that gambling is any venture that has a negative expected value. In other terms, if you can only make money by being lucky, it is a gamble. Before we get too far, let's dive into expected value.

Expected value can be calculated as the chance that you win multiplied by the reward, minus the chance you lose multiplied by the cost to play. For instance, if we had a fair coin where you get $1 if it's heads and you lose $1 if it's tails, the expected value would be $0, calculated as so:
$1 of winnings * 50% chance of heads - $1 of losses * 50% chance of tails = $0.50 - $.50 = $0.00. 

Now just because the expected value is $0 does not mean you never win or lose. If I flip the coin once, you will either win $1 or lose $1. But as we do more flips and obtain a sufficiently large sample size, the net earnings will approach $0.00. Volume removes luck. 

Calculating the expected value is easy when you know the risk of loss, but stocks do not come with such a percentage. Because of this, we have to calculate an approximation for the expected value. In the process of creating this "model", we will need to rely on some assumptions. But if our assumptions are reasonable, we can be reasonably confident in our conclusion.

Gamblers and skeptics say that the market is gambling. If that's true, betting on everything proportionally should lose. But we find that if you bet on an index fund which invested a little bit in every stock since inception 150 years ago, you would get a positive return of ~9.381%. It may not always be this way year to year. In July 1932, the DOW Jones Industrial Average lost of ~90%.[https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/042115/what-caused-stock-market-crash-1929-preceded-great-depression.asp]. On an annualized basis, that is -547,290%! But months like these are offset by booms, such as April 2020 when the market gained 13.6% in a single month, or ~330% on an annualized basis. So on average, through the highest highs and lowest lows, the market returns around 10%. And because of how easy it is to open an index fund, this is a reasonable benchmark to use as a starting point, because if you advisor can't beat the market, then they're likely a below-average investor.

There are lots of robust metrics you can use, but a solid one is the Sharpe Ratio. Ask each advisor to tell you their Sharpe ratio from a given time period, and compare it to the Sharpe Ratio from the S&P 500, or whatever benchmark you want to use. A Sharpe ratio between 1.0 and 2.0 is generally considered good. A ratio above 2.0 is very good, and above 3.0 is excellent. In any case, the advisor's ratios _must_ be greater than the benchmark, not equal, otherwise you are better off investing in the index fund, which you can do in [less than 13 minutes](link)

Be wary of two tricks:
- Advisors may try to convince u to use a different benchmark. As a rule of thumb, the S&P 500 should be sufficient. 
- Advisors may try to manipulate the time period to show a better ratio. Ensure that the same start and end dates are used when comparing the ratios

If you are fielding proposals from multiple advisors, ask them to provide the historical records of all their trades for the given time period, and give them to opposing advisors. Easiest solution is that a financier does not get paid if they do not perform.
Define performance:

- Greater return than a benchmark or control
  - realized return
  - mitigation of risk
    tricky when one is present, easy when both are present or both are absent

1. realized return is higher than benchmark & volatility/risk is lower than benchmark
   Not only did their expertise yield more money for you, they did it taking on less risk. They adhered to the highest fiduciary standards -- providing reasonable returns and preserving the principal. Pay them their fee

2. Return is lower and volatility is higher
   Not only did they earn you less money, they took on more risk than needed and still lost your money. Run away.

3. Return is higher than benchmark but more risky, or return is lower but less risky
   This is difficult, because you need to sus out whether the difference in return was due to the change in risk. Generally speaking, if you strictly reward higher returns, you incentivize riskier behavior in the short term, despite lower risk, safer bets being a higher net gain on the long term due to compounding interest. A heuristic I would personally use would be that the return should be proportional to the risk, so if my portfolio is 10% more volatile, it would be considering underperformed if the return is anything less than 10% more than the benchmark. Conversely, if my portfolio was 10% less volatile, then the return should be within 10% of the benchmark, otherwise it would be considered underperforming.

When done correctly, the incentives are aligned, and the customer is always better off with the expert than without. As it is, people are living with leaky sinks because they're worried the plumbers are goign to fleece them, or worse, break the rest of their sinks. They'll keep their life savings in a 0.01% APY savings account and remain oblivious to the fact that they're losing money due to inflation.

The main disadvantage of this structure is that 1) an annual timeframe is arbitrarily short, and 2) the cash flows don't always work out with the existing rates. To mitigate this, I would propose a modest contract of 3-5 years, and a higher rate, paid out annually. If they underperform one year, they have the following year to make it up, but they don't get paid because they underperformed.

Why it matters: Trust in the financial system, and the individuals who work in it is paramount. Finance is a human activity, and the less trust that exists between participants, more resources are spent establishing trust, which drives up the cost of transacting. Everything from buying a pizza to buying a home costs more when trust declines.

It also matters for serving the underbanked, who are partially underserved because of systemic problems, but partially underserved because they don't understand the system and they don't trust it. Building a more trusting financial system lowers costs, which can result in making services more accessible for the underbanked, and has the added benefit of enticing the more skeptical of customers. And as more people participate in the system, economies of scale enable costs to be driven down even more, causing a virtuous cycle of sorts.
